The Failure of Visual Watermarks in the Face of AI

JM
Javier Moreno
Lead Security Researcher

For years, visual watermarks have served as a trusted shield for protecting authenticity and copyright on the internet. However, with the rapid, aggressive advancement of generative artificial intelligence, this method has become entirely inadequate.

Today, deep learning models can detect, bypass, or erase these watermarks almost effortlessly—and without compromising image quality. Furthermore, diffusion-based generators are capable of replicating structural patterns and entire styles, effectively obscuring the original provenance of any file.

Since relying on passive visual watermarks is no longer effective at curbing automated editing, cybersecurity must shift toward cryptographic standards for provenance. Ultimately, what matters is validating data through system metadata, rather than relying solely on what is visible to the eye.

Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZK-Proofs): A New Defense for Data Authenticity

BO
Brayan Ojeda
Data Architect

With AI generating fake content everywhere, distinguishing the real from the synthetic has become a major challenge. This is where Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZK-Proofs)—a powerful cryptographic solution—come to the rescue. Essentially, they allow one party to mathematically prove to another that a piece of data or an image is legitimate, without needing to reveal the underlying private information.

In practice, these proofs can certify that a photo or document originated from an actual sensor or camera, without exposing the user's location, personal information, or the internal details of the file.

Integrating this technology into our workflows means we can verify authenticity with 100% certainty, all while safeguarding individual privacy in a digital landscape rife with manipulation.

Biometric Inconsistencies in Deepfakes and Synthetic Content

TS
Team Sekiro
Research Group 2026

Although algorithms used to create deepfakes can be deceptive at first glance, they often leave behind clear traces and biometric inconsistencies when examined closely. While synthetic video tools are highly effective at mimicking facial features, they struggle to replicate finer details of human physiology—such as micro-expressions, natural blinking patterns, or subtle variations in blood flow beneath the skin.

Furthermore, there are spatial and temporal flaws: misaligned gazes, unnatural corneal reflections, or poorly rendered edges around the jawline, all of which reveal the manipulation.

Identifying these subtle biological anomalies provides the technical insights needed to develop automated verification systems and mitigate the impact of fabricated content.
